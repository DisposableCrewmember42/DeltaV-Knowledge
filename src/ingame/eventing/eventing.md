# Basics Of Eventing

As a Curator (or Admin), you have the power to make rounds more interesting or worse.
It is the Curators' job to generally respond to players actions or to put players in interesting positions.

Generally, events fall into two types:

### Reactive
Responding to faxes, prayers or making something appear from a group of players' actions would be a reactive event.
These commonly fall under the Low Impact or Medium Impact event categories. 
Usually, these events are improvised.

### Proactive
Staff create scenarios that players have to interact with, like sending a shuttle to repair for a monetary reward.
The impact these events have on the overall round varies, but they could usually be classified as Medium Impact.

## The Job
In short, your job is to add onto the round with your event.
Make it more interesting, **make it fun for everyone, not just for yourself.**

***Don't be afraid to experiment with ideas, even if they go somewhat poorly.***
A random, small joke you made with your fellow staff might just lead to an overblown cinematic ending for the station, which leaves a memorable round for you and for the players.
It's the main source of enjoyment and goal for any eventer - *making rounds memorable for everyone.*

### Doing It Right

Of course, making a round memorable (in a good way) is incredibly hard. It generally comes down to **forethought, improvisation and restraint.** 
Remember, events that players might mistake for being a normal part of the game are the best kind.

### Forethought
**The main tactic you can use whenever thinking or doing an event, is considering *what the players would do*.** 

Put yourself in the players' shoes:
- What would you do, as a player, if you were faced with this event?
- What about your different characters? 
- What would be the worst case scenario?
- What would be the best case scenario?
- If a certain scenario happened, like one of the key members died of your event, what would happen then?
- Could you, as a player playing security, be able to interact with this event during a crisis? 
  - What about as a scientist? 
  - Maybe there's no crisis?

It seems daunting at first, and you might catch yourself overthinking your plan to the point of not wanting to do it because parts of it might go wrong,
but you shouldn't be afraid to experiment. You need to get a grip on this whole philosophy by constantly asking yourself these questions. 
With enough experience, you will subconsciously take these questions into consideration and become a well adept "storyteller" in no time.

### Improvisation
While you might have an overly detailed plan on how things should go, you have to remember that you're playing a *disaster simulator.* 
Things are unpredictable; they can and will go wrong. You'll be trying to wrangle back some control without overly affecting the natural chaos of the game.

This is why it's generally not advised to write a "script" of what *has* to happen in your event. You will usually end up frustrated for things not going your way, which is incredibly discouraging. While you can control what game mode and scheduler the round has, you cannot predict what players will do with your event. 

Write an event plan of what needs to be done, what things need to be spawned in and what generally is supposed to happen. **However, always leave room for improvisation.** Have some written up scenarios or expectations of what could happen during your event, but be ready to adapt on the fly.

### Restraint

Improvisation is a good skill to have. However, it takes quite a lot of restraint to not go overboard with your ideas.

**When you're eventing, you're practically trying to find the right balance between too little and too much.**

**Too much** is when you:
- Over-gear your characters with ludicrous amount of supplies just because "it's what they realistically would carry", while not taking in account of what they could scrounge up from other departments.
- Seeing your key event figure die due to some understandable reason (like security shooting them for constantly teleporting out of jail due to curator intervention) and angrily or panickingly revive them several times just to have them reach your end goal.
- Spawning an RPG for someone out of nowhere, because either ghost chat or you thought it would be hilarious for them to go out in a blaze of glory.

**Too little** is when you:
- Let your event end early on, because your key figure got hit by a meteor while travelling to the station and you decided not to revive them.
- Abandon your support for an antagonistic group you created when the story reaches its climax. For example: Some cultists try sacrificing the Captain to you for immense power while their doors are getting breached by security, and you don't give them anything for their efforts.
- Don't give appropriate gear to units you're sending, like spawning ERT without hardsuits to help out a spaced station.
- Instantly deny a fax that has a potentially interesting plotline for the player to go off of.

Developing the proper restraint generally comes down to experience. However, there are a some questions you can always ask yourself:
- Will the action I'm about to perform add anything to the story or detract from it?
- Would the players interacting with the event feel my input is fair or reasonable? (Would rejuvenating a dead, cuffed prisoner next to a security officer be fair to the officer?)
- Does the player really need your help or can they solve the problem by themselves?
